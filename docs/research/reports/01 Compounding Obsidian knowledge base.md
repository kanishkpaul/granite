# Build a Workshop, Not a Warehouse

An Obsidian vault compounds when it regularly holds **your own claims, written in your own words and linked to what you already know, and something you produce keeps pulling those notes back out.** Folder schemes, plugin stacks and AI pipelines matter much less. The working setup in November 2026 is close to what Obsidian CEO Steph Ango and other long-term users run. It has a flat vault with a few folders sorted by note *type*. A small set of flat YAML properties is queried through the core Bases plugin. Capture is fast, through daily or timestamped notes and Web Clipper. Clippings and AI material sit apart from your own notes. Every link is a judgment you made. Above that sits a tiered review rhythm and a low-bar output habit such as weekly notes, TILs or project memos. AI now has a real role: semantic retrieval, link *suggestions*, vault linting and summarizing sources. Applying it to your evergreen notes removes the generation step that makes a vault worth having. All of this rests on modest evidence. **No controlled or longitudinal study of Zettelkasten, PARA, LYT or evergreen notes exists.** The case comes from lab cognitive science (generation, elaboration, spacing) plus practitioner reports, and survivors write most of those. Expect slow payoff. One estimate puts Luhmann's output at about one book written per hundred read. The first year has to be cheap enough that you don't quit before the network gets dense enough to surprise you.

## Compounding comes from authorship and output, not from which method you pick

The well-known methods split into two families. Thinking systems place value in rewriting and linking ideas: Luhmann and Ahrens's Zettelkasten, Andy Matuschak's evergreen notes, Ango's bottom-up vault, and Nick Milo's Linking Your Thinking with Maps of Content. Matuschak's notes should be **atomic, concept-oriented, densely linked, associative rather than hierarchical, and written for yourself**, and he frames the goal as "better thinking", not better note-taking ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes)). Retrieval systems place value in finding things fast for current work. Tiago Forte's PARA files by actionability (Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives) and argues against broad subject folders ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/para/)). Johnny.Decimal files by stable type in a fixed tree at most two levels deep ([johnnydecimal.com](https://johnnydecimal.com/concepts/areas-categories/)). These two goals don't compete, and the evidence points to one variable that matters more than method choice.

That variable is a **production loop**. The success stories (Luhmann, Matuschak, Simon Willison, an academic who credits his vault with just over 50,000 words in 2025) all have regular output pulling notes through processing ([lateworld](https://lateworld.substack.com/p/failing-upward)). The failures describe storage with no such pull. Joan Westenberg deleted about 10,000 notes going back to 2015. Her diagnosis covers accumulation treated as a virtue, an archive that became a "mausoleum", and sorting and distilling deferred to a future self who never showed up ([Westenberg](https://westenberg.beehiiv.com/p/i-deleted-my-second-brain)). One Obsidian user audited 3.5 years of notes and found **21 permanent notes and 182 of 281 fleeting notes never processed**. Their recent 90-day inflow was 79 clippings, 142 AI conversations and summaries, and one fleeting note of their own. Their conclusion was that tags and links never produced a permanent note, because the missing step was writing their own claims ([sho7650](https://dev.to/sho7650/my-obsidian-vault-made-21-permanent-notes-in-35-years-so-i-redesigned-it-2229)). Christian Tietze named the pattern in 2014 as the **collector's fallacy**: having material at hand feels like knowing it, and collecting rewards itself ([zettelkasten.de](https://zettelkasten.de/posts/collectors-fallacy/)).

The cognitive science supports the steps the thinking methods insist on and rates others poorly. A meta-analysis of 445 effect sizes found that generated material is remembered about **9% better than material that is only read, roughly d = 0.40**, though mostly with word lists and not full notes ([Bertsch et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)). Dunlosky and colleagues' review rated **practice testing and distributed practice as high utility, and highlighting, rereading and summarization as low** ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). The popular claim that longhand notes beat typing because they force paraphrase failed a direct replication and meta-analysis ([Urry et al. 2021](https://www.pubmed.gov/33539228/)).

| Practice | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| Rewrite an idea in your own words as a standalone note | Supported by the generation effect and generative learning ([Fiorella & Mayer summary](https://www.umpi.edu/ctl/?p=5173)) |
| Link a new note to existing ones and say *why* | Supported: elaboration, integrating new material with prior knowledge |
| Revisit notes on a schedule, ideally recalling before rereading | Supported: spacing and retrieval practice, both rated high utility |
| Progressive summarization layers 1–3 (highlight, then bold) | Weak for learning. It may still help your future self find things |
| Atomic notes with concept titles | Untested. A reasonable design argument for precision and reuse |

Two practical rules follow. Spend your processing effort where the evidence is: write a note in your own words for ideas you would actually use or argue with. Keep literature notes to a citation and a few points in your own words. Process fleeting notes within days or delete them. Forte's "process it when you next touch it" rule and the Zettelkasten "process it now" rule can coexist ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)). The rewrite in your own words is the step you can't skip. Second, set expectations. Tietze and Edmund Gröpl reconstruct Luhmann's slip-box at about 67,000 notes plus 15,000 bibliographic entries. That works out to roughly **100 books read per book written, about 1%**, which Tietze calls "an interesting number to keep you humble" ([Tietze 2026](https://christiantietze.de/posts/2026/03/zettelkasten-productivity-books-written-per-books-ingested/)). Most of what you capture will never reach output, and that isn't failure. Skeptics report years before payoff, or none after ten months of daily work ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=22492381)). That is the strongest argument for keeping the system cheap to run.

## Sort folders by note type and let links and properties carry the topics

Experienced users converge on a hybrid. Topics live in links and properties. Folders are few and shallow and sort by what *kind* of file a note is, never by subject, because a note fits only one folder but belongs to many topics. Ango goes furthest: "few folders, no nested sub-folders, and little use of the file explorer," with navigation by quick switcher, backlinks and in-note links, and written rules including "Avoid folders for organization" and "Avoid multiple vaults" ([Ango](https://stephango.com/vault)). Sébastien Dubois reaches the same principle from the other side. File by type, because a note's type doesn't change when your interests do. Keep folders and type tags deliberately redundant, because terminal tools, git and AI agents read file paths but not Obsidian tags ([dsebastien, Oct 2026](https://www.dsebastien.net/structure-a-findable-obsidian-vault-folders-links-dates-and-daily-notes/)). Milo's ACE model (Atlas for knowledge, Calendar for time, Efforts for action) is the same idea under other names, and his rule is that "structure must be earned" ([LYT](https://blog.linkingyourthinking.com/notes/ace-folder-framework)). Long-time users in a forum thread on 6,000–15,000-note vaults report that vaults got unruly when *small* and easier to manage at scale. One moved most documents out of the vault and kept only evergreen notes inside ([Obsidian forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/for-those-with-huge-vaults-anything-you-d-do-differently-or-wish-you-d-known-before-starting/99754)).

| Location | Contents | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Root (or one `Notes/`) | Your own writing: evergreen notes, essays, journal entries, timestamped captures | Ango's root holds "notes he wrote or that relate directly to him" ([Ango](https://stephango.com/vault)) |
| `References/` | One note per outside thing (book, person, paper, tool), named by its real title | Holds properties, ratings and your reactions; journal notes link out to it |
| `Clippings/` | Other people's writing from Web Clipper, Readwise, etc. | Keeps source material apart from your claims (the raw-vs-own split) |
| `Daily/` | `YYYY-MM-DD.md` (weekly `YYYY-Www`, monthly `YYYY-MM` if used) | ISO names make `file:2026-10` return a whole month ([dsebastien](https://www.dsebastien.net/structure-a-findable-obsidian-vault-folders-links-dates-and-daily-notes/)) |
| `Attachments/`, `Templates/` | Images, PDFs, audio; property-scaffold templates | Admin folders, invisible in daily use |
| Optional `Projects/` and `Archive/` | Active work, if there is a lot of it; old imports and abandoned projects | The useful part of PARA; archiving keeps material searchable without processing it |

Properties and Bases are what make a flat vault work, and they are the main reason advice from before 2024 about deep folders or tag hierarchies is out of date. Properties arrived in Obsidian 1.4.5 in August 2023 and are still stored as plain YAML frontmatter ([changelog](https://obsidian.md/changelog/2023-08-31-desktop-v1.4.5)). Bases became available to everyone in 1.9.10 in August 2025. It provides table, list, card, map and (since 1.14.0, September 2026) kanban views over those properties. "All Bases data is stored in your local Markdown files and their properties," so a broken or deleted base loses only a view ([Obsidian Help: Bases](https://obsidian.md/help/bases); [changelog](https://obsidian.md/changelog/)). Ango's main organizer is a `categories` property whose values are *links* to category notes. Each category note embeds a base and works as the overview page. This combines the hand-built Map of Content with the live query. His schema rules are short, reusable names (`start`, not `start-date`), plural categories and tags, and **list types wherever a value might ever hold more than one item** ([Ango](https://stephango.com/vault)). The docs add two constraints. Nested properties are unsupported, and the singular keys `tag`, `alias` and `cssclass` stopped working in 1.9 ([Obsidian Help: Properties](https://obsidian.md/help/properties)). A starter schema built from Ango and Dubois:

```yaml
---
categories: ["[[Books]]"]        # list of links; each category note embeds a base
created: 2026-11-03
tags: [drafts]                   # few, flat, plural; workflow states only
aliases: [Alt name, ACRONYM]
author: ["[[Ted Chiang]]"]       # per-category fields: author, url, rating, status, start, end
up: ["[[Fiction MOC]]"]          # optional parent pointer, queryable without folders
---
```

The rule for deciding which tool to use comes straight from these sources. **If it is a thing you might write about (person, book, concept, project), link it. If it is an attribute you will filter or sort by, make it a property. If it is a temporary workflow state, a tag is fine.** Practitioners keep naming tag sprawl and deep tag nesting as long-term regrets, and one forum veteran calls "tagging without organization" a Zettelkasten "deadly sin" ([Obsidian forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/for-those-with-huge-vaults-anything-you-d-do-differently-or-wish-you-d-known-before-starting/99754)). For names, give your own notes claim-style titles that work "like APIs": a sharp phrase you can cite by handle ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_note_titles_are_like_APIs)). Name reference notes by their real-world title. Use ISO date prefixes only for time-bound notes such as Ango's `YYYY-MM-DD HHmm` timestamped captures ([Ango](https://stephango.com/vault)). Obsidian rewrites links when you rename a note, which is what makes descriptive titles safe. Git, scripts and other editors won't rewrite them, so names matter more the more outside tools touch the vault.

For linking, Ango's advice is to "link profusely," link the first mention of anything, and leave unresolved links as "breadcrumbs for future connections" ([Ango](https://stephango.com/vault)). Hold off on MOCs and index notes until the **"mental squeeze point"**, when a cluster gets too big to keep in your head ([LYT glossary](https://blog.linkingyourthinking.com/notes/lyt-glossary)). Give each link in an MOC a sentence of context, and generate MOCs from properties where you can ([dsebastien](https://www.dsebastien.net/structure-a-findable-obsidian-vault-folders-links-dates-and-daily-notes/)). Users who hand-built hub notes everywhere report that keeping them current became a chore ([pjordan](https://pjordan.substack.com/p/obsidian-zettelkasten-and-the-holy)). There is one real tension. Some users found that capturing and linking everything left them with "meaningless" connections between trivial notes. The likely difference is that Ango's links come as a by-product of writing, while theirs became a maintenance goal in their own right. Last, follow Ango's **"file over app"** principle ("Apps are ephemeral, but your files have a chance to last") ([Ango](https://stephango.com/file-over-app)). Keep core content in standard Markdown and flat YAML. Avoid inline `key:: value` fields, DataviewJS blocks and plugin-only syntax that renders only at runtime, and treat `.base` files as views you can replace. The `.base` format already had one breaking change, in 1.9.2 ([Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/news/obsidian-192-brings-breaking-changes-ui-improvements-and-several-bug-fixes)).

## Core features now do most of what plugins used to, so install few

Obsidian's core absorbed a lot in 2025–2026. As a result, the most durable setup uses the fewest plugins. Beyond Bases, the important additions are the **official CLI** in v1.12.4 in February 2026, "for scripting, automation, and integration with external tools," with a dedicated binary since 1.12.7 ([changelog 1.12.4](https://obsidian.md/changelog/2026-02-27-desktop-v1.12.4/); [1.12.7](https://obsidian.md/changelog/2026-03-23-desktop-v1.12.7/)). The 1.14.x line added iOS Quick Capture, opening Markdown files from outside the vault, and a fix for a metadata-cache memory leak. Version 1.15 in October–November 2026 added **URI/CLI commands to append or prepend under a heading** and faster plugin loading ([changelog](https://obsidian.md/changelog/)). Those append commands give iOS Shortcuts, scripts and agents a native, scriptable way to capture into a daily note. The official **Web Clipper** is the standard intake. It is free, runs on all major browsers including iOS Safari, offers per-site templates that map page metadata to properties, a highlighter, and an "Interpreter" that extracts data with natural-language prompts, and it saves locally with no usage tracking ([Obsidian Help: Web Clipper](https://obsidian.md/help/web-clipper)). Clipped images can stay as remote URLs until you download them. Download them into `Attachments/` so clippings don't rot when the URLs die ([Obsidian Help](https://help.obsidian.md/web-clipper/capture)).

**Dataview versus Bases** is the biggest plugin decision, and it now favors Bases for new work. Dataview is not dead. A new maintainer, holroy, shipped releases 0.5.68 and 0.5.70 in spring, most likely 2026 since the releases page shows no year ([GitHub](https://github.com/blacksmithgu/obsidian-dataview/releases)). It still handles queries Bases can't. Its output, though, shows only in rendered view, not in backlinks, the graph, git diffs or an AI agent reading raw files ([dsebastien](https://www.dsebastien.net/the-complete-guide-to-dataview-in-obsidian/)). Bases is core, reads the same YAML, and the core team shipped kanban, grouping and reorderable filters within about two months in late 2026. A "Dataview to Bases" migration plugin exists, with one catch: Bases always filters, then sorts, then limits ([plugin listing](https://community.obsidian.md/plugins/dataview-to-bases)). The practical rule is **new structured views go in Bases, and keep Dataview only for what Bases can't express.** Practitioners widely recommend Templater, QuickAdd, Periodic Notes, Linter, Obsidian Git, Spaced Repetition, Strange New Worlds, Zotero Integration and Readwise Official. The research did not verify their 2026 maintenance status, so check each one's latest commit before you depend on it. Prefer plugins whose output stays plain text if the plugin dies.

Restraint about plugins is also how you keep the vault fast. Forum reports put core Obsidian comfortably at 10,000–20,000 notes. The slowdowns that do get reported come with plugin load: 20,000 notes and 70 plugins hung, while 13,000 notes and about 60 plugins ran fine ([forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/obsidian-hanging/102330); [forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/slow-obsidian-startup/41480)). Plugins run on a single-threaded UI, so one slow callback can lag your typing ([forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/slow-typing-to-mars-and-back/66543)). For sync, **Obsidian Sync is the only officially supported option**. The help docs warn against iCloud on Windows and against running two sync services on one vault ([Obsidian Help: Sync](https://obsidian.md/help/sync-notes)), and the forums have repeated reports of iCloud silently overwriting files or leaving 0-byte files ([forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/icloud-data-loss-issue-sync-conflict-handling/113584)). Obsidian Sync has its own loss reports ([forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/constant-data-loss-between-mobile-and-desktop/66663)), so treat **sync and backup as separate jobs**. Run Obsidian Sync, or Syncthing if you have an always-on device, for live sync. Add git through Obsidian Git or a cron commit on one desktop for history, plus an off-machine backup. Git stops being optional once an AI agent can write to the vault.

## AI belongs in retrieval and maintenance, not in your evergreen notes

Three AI integration patterns had settled by late 2026. The first is in-app plugins. **Smart Connections** v4 ships a built-in local embedding model, so it needs no API key and works offline and on mobile. It shows semantically related notes as you write, and you drag one in to create a link ([GitHub](https://github.com/brianpetro/obsidian-smart-connections)). Its license moved from GPL to a source-available "Smart Plugins License"; only one source covers the timing ([PromptQuorum](https://www.promptquorum.com/power-local-llm/smart-connections-review)). **Copilot for Obsidian** provides chat and an agent mode that runs Claude Code, Codex or OpenCode inside Obsidian on desktop, free with your own key or local models ([GitHub](https://github.com/logancyang/obsidian-copilot)). Reviewers often run both, Smart Connections for discovery and Copilot for conversation. The second pattern is external agents working on the vault folder directly. Ango's MIT-licensed **kepano/obsidian-skills** repository, with about 50,000 stars, gives Claude Code agent skills for Obsidian Markdown, Bases, JSON Canvas, the CLI and defuddle web extraction, installed into `.claude` at the vault root ([GitHub](https://github.com/kepano/obsidian-skills)). Combined with the official CLI, this is now the most native agent route. Some practitioners also keep a `CLAUDE.md` at the vault root that states the vault's conventions ([Reitz](https://kennethreitz.org/essays/2026-03-06-obsidian_vaults_and_claude_code)). The third pattern is MCP servers for Claude Desktop and similar clients. Either point a generic filesystem MCP at the vault, or use an Obsidian-specific server through the Local REST API plugin. The second option adds a fragile chain of plugin, server and client, and setup guides tell you to back up with git before granting write access ([Playbooks](https://playbooks.com/mcp/stevenstavrakis-obsidian)).

Andrej Karpathy's April 2026 "LLM knowledge base" post turned a fourth idea into a movement. Sources go into an immutable `raw/` folder. An LLM "compiles" them into an interlinked wiki of summaries and concept pages, navigated through index files instead of a vector database, and runs "linting/health checks" over it. Answers to queries get filed back in. In his words, "You rarely ever write or edit the wiki manually; it's the domain of the LLM" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/data/karpathy-shares-llm-knowledge-base-architecture-that-bypasses-rag-with-an)). His version covered about 100 articles and 400,000 words. Note what this pattern does: it keeps the linked-notes artifact and removes the human generation step, which is the step the evidence in the first section supports. That makes it a strong design for a *reference* corpus on a domain you need to query, and a weak one for building your own understanding. The counter-movement makes this argument directly. The essay "Keep AI Out of Your Obsidian Vault" argues that "AI summaries become noise, and you lose the thinking that comes from making your own links." Commenters on HN added a compounding-error risk: agents write confident claims that later sessions treat as fact ([ssp.sh](https://www.ssp.sh/brain/using-obsidian-with-ai/); [HN via Zeli](https://zeli.app/story/49450898)). Ango, who wrote the skills repo, still refuses to let an LLM do his reviews: "Don't delegate understanding" ([Ango](https://stephango.com/vault)). The sho7650 redesign shows the practical middle ground. A human writes the claims and the reasoning. AI only fact-checks, offers the strongest counterargument and suggests related notes, and never rewrites the body ([sho7650](https://dev.to/sho7650/my-obsidian-vault-made-21-permanent-notes-in-35-years-so-i-redesigned-it-2229)). Jorge Arango calls the same role an "amanuensis": a collaborator, not a ghostwriter ([Arango](https://jarango.com/2026/03/27/robots-in-the-garden/)).

| Use AI for | Keep AI out of |
|---|---|
| Semantic search and "related notes" while writing (local embeddings for sensitive vaults) | Writing evergreen or permanent notes in your voice |
| Link *suggestions* you accept one at a time | Bulk auto-linking, which fills backlinks and the graph with shallow edges |
| Linting: orphans, broken links, property normalization, duplicates, stale-note reports | Silent whole-file rewrites by agents with write access |
| Summaries of clipped sources, labeled as such, in `Clippings/` or a separate AI folder or vault | Mixing AI text into your notes without labels, where later agents treat it as fact |
| Q&A and audits over the vault (what's stale, what's unprocessed, what you keep returning to) | Sending private vaults to cloud models without deciding to |

Four guardrails make this safe. Put provenance in the file: AI material goes in its own folder or a separate vault and carries a `generated: true` or `author: ai` property. Commit to git before every agent session and review changes with `git diff`. Prefer the CLI or section-aware edits over whole-file rewrites. Use local embeddings (Smart Connections' built-in model, or Copilot with Ollama or LM Studio) for anything you wouldn't paste into a cloud chat.

## A three-tier review rhythm and a low-bar output habit keep the loop running

The habits that hold up over years are tiered. Capture is frequent and tiny. Consolidation is regular but short. Maintenance is rare and lazy. Ango never writes in his daily notes. He captures each thought as a timestamped note that links to the day, compiles the fragments every few days, reviews those monthly, reviews the monthly reviews yearly with a "40 questions" template, and every few months uses the random-note hotkey and a shallow local graph to revisit old ideas and add missing links ([Ango](https://stephango.com/vault)). Dubois writes captures straight into the daily note instead, then splits them into named atomic notes during a weekly review ([dsebastien](https://www.dsebastien.net/structure-a-findable-obsidian-vault-folders-links-dates-and-daily-notes/)). Either works. What matters is that capture costs nothing and something you schedule turns fragments into titled, linked notes in your own words. Matuschak's advice is to process collected material "in short cycles" before it piles up ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zQm6XAB3XXrXLHzF7gahpJ2)). One warning: a ritual can run without producing any thinking. One user's daily-note KPI section got filled in every day while the "Thoughts in my mind" section stayed empty ([sho7650](https://dev.to/sho7650/my-obsidian-vault-made-21-permanent-notes-in-35-years-so-i-redesigned-it-2229)). Judge a routine by what it produces, not by whether it ran.

The output habit is what actually closes the loop. Matuschak notes that note-writing gives "weak feedback": bad notes cause no immediate pain, so failure goes unnoticed for years ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Note-writing_practices_provide_weak_feedback)). Low-bar publishing formats create that feedback. Willison's TILs set the bar at "did I just learn something?" and produced **148 posts across 43 topics in the first year**, avoiding the unfinished drafts that pressure to be original creates ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2021/May/2/one-year-of-tils)). In 2026 he wired those notes back into his blog archive and search ([Willison 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Mar/23/beats-now-have-notes/)). The same fix applies inside a vault. Define a permanent note as "correct as of the verification date, and revisable later". Add a "Today's one" slot to the daily note. Set a concrete target, such as eight verified permanent notes in four weeks ([sho7650](https://dev.to/sho7650/my-obsidian-vault-made-21-permanent-notes-in-35-years-so-i-redesigned-it-2229)). For a backlog, **declare bankruptcy: move it to `Archive/` and start writing forward.** Grinding through hundreds of stale captures, or deleting everything as Westenberg did, are both versions of the reset trap. An archive gives you the fresh start and keeps the material searchable.

Nobody has established metrics for whether a vault pays off, but some proxies hold up. Count self-written notes created or revised per month. Track the mix of inflow: your own writing against clippings and AI material. If the 142-to-1 ratio in the audit above sounds familiar, that is the diagnosis. Track output per input, Gröpl's "how many books have grown out of what I read" ([Tietze 2026](https://christiantietze.de/posts/2026/03/zettelkasten-productivity-books-written-per-books-ingested/)). Track backlog age. An agent such as Claude Code can run this audit quarterly by reading the vault, which is how the sho7650 audit was done. Two more measures are my own inferences, not something the sources tested: how often notes get cited in finished work, and whether you can find what you need in under a minute.

For a newcomer, every source points the same way. **Start minimal and let structure appear when friction calls for it.** In week one, set up one vault with the folders from the table above, a daily note, a capture hotkey and Web Clipper, plus a five-line "rules" note you're allowed to change. Ango says to take "the parts you like" and that "you can always change your rules later" ([Ango](https://stephango.com/vault)). In month one, tie the vault to a single real project or question and start one low-bar output stream. One writer who gave up on Obsidian twice says it finally stuck once they used it for specific tasks ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/gave-up-on-obsidian-twice-before-found-the-setup-that-stuck/)). Another stopped building the "perfect vault" and used it for one thing ([MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/stopped-building-perfect-obsidian-vault-use-it-for-one-thing/)). In quarter one, do the first random-note maintenance pass, build the first base or MOC wherever a cluster has reached the squeeze point, and run the first inflow audit. Add a plugin only when the same pain has come up several times. With fewer than about 100 notes, according to one 10,000-note veteran, you don't need a system at all ([Obsidian forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/for-those-with-huge-vaults-anything-you-d-do-differently-or-wish-you-d-known-before-starting/99754)).

## Conclusion

Late 2026 changed where the bottleneck sits. Upkeep used to be the main barrier to PKM: filing, linking, summarizing and querying. Core Bases, the CLI and agents now make that upkeep close to free. What remains scarce is the one thing those tools can't supply, which is your judgment written down as a claim. The most likely failure for a new vault today is a well-organized, AI-maintained library of material you never thought through, not a messy folder tree. That makes provenance the most important design decision you'll make. Your vault should make it obvious which notes you wrote, which you collected, and which a model generated, and it should keep the first group growing.

The evidence also argues for humility about any specific recipe, this one included. Every method's success stories come from people with a steady output habit, every method's failures come from people without one, and nobody has run the controlled comparison. Choose conventions that are cheap to keep and easy to change. Keep the data in plain files that will outlast any of these tools. Judge the vault by how often your own writing in it gets reused, not by how big or how well connected it is.
